Summary (ELI5)

Imagine you have a huge box of letter tiles. Some letter pairs, like "t" and "h", keep showing up next to each other. You decide to glue those two tiles together into one new tile called "th". You keep gluing the most frequent pairs together until you have a nice set of bigger and bigger tiles. Now, when you see a new word, you can build it using these pre‑glued tiles instead of every single letter. That’s Byte‑Pair Encoding (BPE): it starts with individual characters (or bytes) and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token. The result is a vocabulary of subword pieces that can represent any text—even words the tokenizer has never seen—by falling back to smaller pieces or individual bytes. This makes it perfect for feeding text into language models: the model sees a sequence of integer token IDs instead of raw characters, and the sequence is shorter and more meaningful than a pure character‑level stream.


Use Cases
2.1 Building the Subword Vocabulary for a New Foundation Model

When a team trains a large language model from scratch, they must decide on a fixed vocabulary of tokens before training begins. Raw text contains millions of distinct words, many of which appear only once. A word‑level vocabulary would either explode in size or replace rare words with a generic <unk> token, losing information. BPE solves this by learning a vocabulary of frequent subword units from the training corpus. The algorithm starts with a base alphabet (often 256 bytes for byte‑level BPE) and greedily merges the most frequent adjacent pairs, producing tokens like “ing”, “tion”, or “pre”. Because the vocabulary is learned from the same domain as the model’s training data, it balances coverage and efficiency: common words become single tokens, while rare or unseen words are decomposed into smaller known pieces. This is exactly what GPT‑2, GPT‑3, Llama, and Claude‑family models do—they all rely on a BPE‑style tokenizer trained on a large, deduplicated corpus. The tokenizer becomes a permanent artefact of the model, and its merge rules determine the “fertility” (tokens per word) and the effective context length. A well‑trained BPE vocabulary can reduce sequence length by 3–5× compared to character‑level tokenization, directly lowering compute cost and memory footprint during training and inference.
2.2 Handling Rare Words, Code, and Multilingual Text via Byte‑Level Fallback

Real‑world text is messy: it contains typos, URLs, emoji, chemical formulas, and code with exotic identifiers. A character‑level BPE tokenizer still requires every character to be in its initial alphabet; if it encounters a Unicode code point it has never seen, it must emit an <unk> token, breaking lossless round‑tripping. Byte‑level BPE (BBPE) eliminates this problem by operating on UTF‑8 bytes instead of Unicode characters. Every possible string—no matter the language or script—is just a sequence of bytes from the fixed set of 256. During training, BBPE learns merges over byte sequences, so common byte patterns become tokens, but any unseen byte can still be represented. This guarantees full coverage: no Out-Of-Vocabulary (OOV) is possible. For code, BBPE learns tokens like def, return, () and indentation patterns; for multilingual text, it learns language‑specific byte sequences for CJK, Arabic, or Devanagari scripts. The trade‑off is that byte‑level tokens are less semantically transparent than character‑level tokens, but the guarantee of lossless encoding for arbitrary Unicode makes BBPE the de‑facto standard for production LLM tokenizers.
2.3 Controlling Sequence Length / Efficiency Trade‑off via Merge Rules

The number of merge operations (or equivalently, the vocabulary size) is a critical hyperparameter. If you stop merging early, you get a small vocabulary but long sequences because words are split into many pieces. If you merge too aggressively, you get a large vocabulary and short sequences, but many tokens appear only once or twice during training, wasting model capacity. BPE lets you dial this trade‑off explicitly: you choose vocab_size (e.g., 32k, 50k, 100k, 150k) and the trainer performs exactly that many merges (minus special tokens). This directly affects “fertility” (tokens per word): lower fertility means shorter sequences, faster inference, and lower API cost. For example, a custom Kazakh BPE tokenizer with a 32k vocabulary achieved fertility 1.68 tokens per word, which was 3.5× more efficient than GPT‑4’s tokenizer for Kazakh, translating to roughly 72% lower API cost and a 3.5× longer effective context window. Teams building domain‑specific models (legal, medical, code) routinely tune the merge count on a representative corpus to hit a target fertility or compression ratio while keeping the vocabulary within the embedding layer’s budget.
2.4 Low‑Resource and Morphologically Rich Languages

Languages like Sinhala, Finnish, Turkish, or many African languages have rich morphology: a single root can spawn dozens of inflected forms, most of which are rare in any corpus. Word‑level tokenizers suffer badly here, producing huge vocabularies and frequent <unk> tokens. BPE, especially byte‑level BPE, is a natural fit because it can learn productive subword units (prefixes, suffixes, stems) from limited data. In a recent study on Sinhala, researchers adopted a byte‑level BPE strategy aligned with the LLaMA 3.1 architecture, ensuring compatibility at the byte level for a non‑Latin, morphologically rich script. Similarly, SentencePiece (which implements BPE and Unigram) was chosen for African low‑resource languages because it handles agglutinative morphology by breaking words into subword units, enabling better coverage of rare and compound words. The practical benefit is dramatic: a 32k‑vocab BPE tokenizer trained on a modest Sinhala corpus outperformed 200k–250k multilingual vocabularies by 1.3–2× on fertility, while keeping the model’s embedding matrix small.
2.5 Code and Logging Text Generation

Source code and log messages are full of identifiers, punctuation, and formatting that never appear in natural language corpora. A tokenizer trained only on English web text will fragment code into many tiny pieces, wasting context and slowing down code‑generation models. BPE trained on a code‑heavy corpus learns tokens that respect programming syntax: common keywords (function, const, return), operators (==, !=, ->), indentation sequences, and frequently used variable‑name fragments. This reduces the number of tokens needed to represent a code snippet and improves the model’s ability to generate syntactically valid code. In logging text generation, for example, BPE helps handle out‑of‑vocabulary tokens in source code and log templates, which are a major source of errors for neural models. OpenAI’s tiktoken and HuggingFace’s tokenizers both provide BPE implementations widely used for code models; the technical card notes that Claude uses a custom BPE tokenizer “optimized for code and multilingual coverage”.
2.6 Domain‑Specific Chatbots and Conversational Agents

A chatbot for a specialized domain (medical, legal, financial, or a low‑resource language) must understand terminology that is rare in general‑purpose corpora. If the tokenizer was trained on generic web text, critical terms like “myocardial infarction” or “force majeure” may be split into many subword pieces, increasing sequence length and making it harder for the model to learn the concept as a single unit. Training a domain‑adapted BPE tokenizer on in‑domain text solves this. For example, a Bangla chatbot built on GPT‑Neo used an optimized BPE tokenizer to significantly improve language‑processing efficiency. The tokenizer learns domain‑specific merges, so key phrases become single tokens, shortening the input and allowing the model to attend to more context. This is a cheap and effective adaptation step: you do not need to retrain the whole LLM; you only retrain the tokenizer and re‑embed the vocabulary (or initialize new embeddings from the old ones). The technical card explicitly lists “Building the subword vocabulary for a new foundation model” and “Handling rare words, code, and multilingual text” as core scenarios.
2.7 Machine Translation for Rare and Compound Words

In machine translation (MT), rare words are a classic bottleneck. A word‑level MT system must map every source word to a target word; if a word was not seen during training, it cannot be translated. BPE was originally popularized in neural MT precisely to solve this: Sennrich et al. (2016) showed that encoding rare words as sequences of subword units allows the model to translate them compositionally. For example, an unseen compound like “unhappiness” can be segmented into un, happi, ness, and each piece has been seen in other contexts. This is especially important for morphologically rich languages and for domain‑specific translation (medical, legal). The technical card lists “Handling rare words … via byte‑level fallback” as a use case, and the references include the seminal BPE‑for‑NMT paper. In practice, BPE‑based subword segmentation is used in production MT systems for languages like Kashmiri‑English, where SentencePiece BPE reduces vocabulary size while capturing prefixes, suffixes, and inflections.
2.8 Sentiment Analysis and Text Classification in Low‑Resource Settings

Sentiment analysis models are often deployed in languages or domains where labeled data is scarce. A word‑level model trained on a small dataset will overfit to the training vocabulary and fail on unseen words. BPE provides a compact, fixed vocabulary that generalizes better: even if a sentiment‑bearing word was never seen, its subword pieces (e.g., a negative prefix or suffix) may carry sentiment. SentencePiece with BPE was chosen for sentiment analysis in African low‑resource languages because it effectively handles agglutinative and morphologically rich languages, enabling better handling of rare, compound, and out‑of‑vocabulary words. The tokenizer can be trained on unlabeled text from the target domain (e.g., social media, product reviews) and then frozen for the downstream classifier. This reduces the model’s embedding size and improves robustness without requiring massive labeled data.
2.9 Medical and Clinical Entity Recognition

Clinical notes are full of abbreviations, misspellings, and rare disease names. A tokenizer trained on general English will fragment terms like “HbA1c” or “myocardial” into many pieces, making it hard for a named‑entity‑recognition (NER) model to learn them as units. BPE trained on a clinical corpus learns medically relevant subword units, improving both tokenization efficiency and downstream NER performance. In one study on ICD‑10 medical entity recognition, BPE was applied to reduce dimensionality and handle rare words, with tokenization cardinalities of 500 and 2033 for the ICD‑10 and French corpora respectively. The same principle applies to radiology reports, pathology notes, and clinical trial descriptions: a domain‑trained BPE vocabulary shortens sequences, reduces Out-Of-Vocabulary (OOV), and lets the model focus on learning entity boundaries rather than character‑level spelling.
2.10 Digital Ink and Handwriting Recognition

Text is not always typed; online handwriting produces a sequence of pen coordinates and strokes. Traditional word‑level tokenizers cannot handle this modality. Tokink, a recent BPE tokenizer designed specifically for digital ink, adapts the BPE paradigm to stroke sequences, enabling handwritten text recognition (HTR) and generation (HTG). The same core idea applies: start with primitive units (strokes or stroke fragments), repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pairs into larger units, and build a vocabulary of “ink tokens”. This demonstrates the generality of BPE beyond natural‑language text: any sequential data with recurring patterns can benefit from greedy pair merging.
